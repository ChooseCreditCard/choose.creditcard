Virtual cards & tap-to-pay
— how “invisible” card payments work.

Modern cards rarely leave your wallet. Instead, you use virtual PANs, device tokens and tap-to-pay through phones and watches. This guide explains what is actually stored where, how transactions are authorised, and what to look for in your card app.

Neutral, educational overview. No issuer has paid to appear in this guide.

What is a “virtual card”?

A virtual card is a card number that only exists in software. It is linked to your main account, but can have its own number, expiry and security code. Typical use-cases:

  • Online shopping without exposing your physical card number
  • Single-use numbers for one-off purchases
  • Merchant-locked numbers for subscriptions
  • Temporary cards while you wait for a physical replacement

Some banks treat the virtual card as a separate “token”; others show it as part of one card profile.

How tap-to-pay and tokenisation actually work

When you add a card to a wallet (such as Apple Pay or Google Pay), the real card number is swapped for a “device token”. This is a card-network issued number that only works from that device and wallet.

1. Provisioning

Your bank or issuer checks the device, then approves a token for that specific phone or watch.

2. Token in use

When you tap, the terminal sees the token, not your underlying card number.

3. Mapping

The card network maps the token back to your real card in the background and routes the authorisation.

This design means a compromised terminal or merchant typically only sees the token, which is less useful if stolen than a raw card number.

What to look for in your card app

Card apps and wallets often expose useful controls that many people never touch. Typical options:

  • Freeze/unfreeze the physical card instantly
  • Separate limits for contactless, online and ATM usage
  • Per-merchant or per-region restrictions
  • Virtual card numbers dedicated to one shop or subscription
  • Push notifications for every transaction

These settings do not change the basic legal protections, but can reduce practical fraud risk.
