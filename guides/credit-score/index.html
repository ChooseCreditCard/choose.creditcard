Credit score, utilisation & approval
— how card issuers read your file.

This guide explains, in neutral terms, how credit scores are typically constructed, which patterns issuers often favour or dislike, and why two people with the same income can get very different card offers. It is not personal financial advice and does not cover country-specific scoring formulas.

Educational overview only. For exact rules, always check local credit bureaus and issuer documentation.

What a credit score usually measures

Different countries and bureaus use different scales, but most credit scores are models that try to predict how likely you are to repay on time. Common input categories include:

  • Payment history – late or missed payments in the past.
  • Utilisation – how much of your available revolving credit you use.
  • Length of history – how long accounts have been open.
  • Mix of credit – cards, loans, overdrafts, etc.
  • New applications – recent hard checks and new accounts.

Scores are not moral judgements. They are statistical estimates built from past population data.

Utilisation and patterns issuers tend to notice

“Utilisation” usually describes how much of your available revolving credit is in use. For example, using 2 000 of a 10 000 limit equals 20 %. Models often care more about patterns than single days:

High constant utilisation

Running close to the limit on several cards over time can be interpreted as higher risk.

Large swings

Rapid jumps up and down may signal stress, even if you do not miss payments.

Low, stable usage

Small, predictable use and on-time repayment is often seen as more favourable.

None of this guarantees approval or rejection. Each issuer has its own internal models, risk appetite and local regulatory constraints.

How card approval often works (high level)

When you apply for a card, the issuer usually pulls data from a credit bureau and combines it with the information you provide in the application form. Typical building blocks include:

  • Credit bureau data (existing accounts, limits, payment record)
  • Declared income and housing situation (e.g. rent vs. own)
  • Existing internal relationship with the bank, if any
  • Regulatory caps, such as debt-to-income thresholds where applicable

The outcome may be: approved as requested, approved with a lower limit, or declined. Sometimes you can request that the issuer reassesses the application based on updated information or documentation.
